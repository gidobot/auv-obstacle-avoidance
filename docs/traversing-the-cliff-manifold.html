Traversing the Cliff Manifold

The seafloor defines the ideal survey trajectory: hold the imaging altitude for as much of the transect as the vehicle's speed and clearance limits allow. Reactive altitude following achieves that wherever the terrain is followable. This is a controller for where it is not — steep faces that cannot be tracked while moving, and edges that must be crossed before the vehicle may descend — and it flies very nearly the trajectory the terrain permits.

01

The claim

For a fixed-altitude survey the objective is in-band line: the distance of the transect flown within a tight band of the imaging altitude. The terrain sets a ceiling on it. The contribution is a controller that reaches very nearly that ceiling online, from sparse causal observation, over terrain rugose enough that altitude following alone cannot.

Reactive altitude following already tracks smoothly varying terrain well under limited observability, and that is not claimed here. What it cannot do is handle two exceptions. Faces steeper than the vehicle's speed ratio cannot be tracked at all while moving forward. And a descending edge cannot be followed down until the tail has crossed it, which requires acting on a feature that is no longer beneath the vehicle.

Two mechanisms cover those exceptions, and both are forced rather than chosen:

Sequencing — suspending forward motion until the vertical error closes. Required because a vehicle can hold the band while surging only where the terrain gradient is at most vertical_speed / survey_speed; beyond it, surging necessarily leaves the band, and every metre flown out of band is a metre of transect not surveyed.

Commitment discharged by geometry — a manoeuvre held until the vehicle has passed the feature, not until the observation lapses or a timer expires. Required because the release condition is a distance: the peak plus standoff plus vehicle length. It cannot be a duration, and not merely because terrain varies — sequencing zeroes forward speed, so elapsed time and distance travelled decouple by construction. A time-based commitment is structurally incompatible with a sequencing controller.

Together they yield a policy with a clean characterisation (§5): surge if and only if the vehicle is in band, or forward motion is required to clear a feature before it may descend. That is the deployed mode structure. Measured against that ceiling the controller holds 67.5% of it, covers the terrain's compulsory ground to the metre, and loses 11.5 m where the terrain required nothing — a loss that oracle perception barely touches, which puts the remaining work in the standoff policy rather than in the sensing.

Scope, stated up front. One mission class: line-survey transects flown by a hover-capable vehicle over reef slopes, planned on a 2-D manifold valid while heading is roughly constant. Hover capability is what makes zero-speed vertical correction available at all; the straight-transect assumption is what makes the projection sound. A 3-D control law would be a different design, and §7 says what would change. This is a boundary on the claim, not an apology.

02

The environment

Coral reef slopes, where vertical profiles, canyons, overhangs, bommies and pervasive small-scale variability sit inside the survey corridor. Imaging from a fixed altitude puts the vehicle within that structure rather than safely above it, so terrain is a collision hazard rather than a mapping nuisance — and the hazards are a continuous property of the seabed, not isolated features to be routed around one at a time.

Structure is dense and local. Relief varies on scales comparable to the vehicle, so the seabed beneath the hull is not described by the reading directly under it. A command derived from a single nadir altitude puts one end of the vehicle into terrain the other end never saw.

The feature stops being observable mid-manoeuvre. Downward Janus beams and the altimeter cannot see a crest above the vehicle, and the forward sonar's return is lost during a climb — yet tail clearance requires the manoeuvre to continue past a feature the vehicle can no longer sense.

Much of the terrain is not followable. At the deployed speeds the followable gradient is 1.0, or 45°. Reef faces routinely exceed it, and no tuning of an altitude follower recovers the band across them.

03

The representation makes sparse sensing sufficient

The platform senses through five rays per cycle — three Janus beams, an altimeter, a forward sonar. That is far too sparse to fill a volume: at 8 Hz it would take roughly four hours of ray endpoints merely to touch each cell of the equivalent 3-D grid once, by which time the vehicle is long gone. Collapsing occupancy onto the vehicle-relative vertical plane, and reducing it to the upper-envelope polyline the planner consumes, is what makes the sensing budget sufficient. Every return lands in the plane the survey is flown in.

Sixty times fewer cells, and the whole planning pipeline — stale-voxel clearing, manifold extraction, commanded-depth profile, waypoints — runs in 6.4 µs median on a fully observed map. Against the 5 Hz the vehicle publishes at that is a duty cycle of 0.003%: the representation is free on the embedded target, which is why it runs inside the LCM node rather than on a companion computer.

It is also dense enough to plan on. In steady state 78% of columns carry a real observation and 94% ahead of the nose, falling to 13% immediately after a turn has invalidated the map and it is rebuilding. Columns without an observation are flagged rather than guessed at, so the avoidance checks skip them instead of triggering on the grid-floor default — the representation carries its own uncertainty rather than pretending to completeness.

Overhangs are a property, not a gap. An upper envelope cannot represent one; what it does instead is take the shallowest occupied voxel per column, so an overhang's underside becomes the manifold and the vehicle is commanded above it. The planner goes over structure, never under. For downward imaging that is arguably correct — the cost is coverage beneath the overhang, not safety. The genuine limitation is the projection itself, valid only while heading is roughly constant (§10).

04

The controller

Five modes. Three command survey speed — ALT_FOLLOW, OBSTACLE_HOLD, TAIL_CLEAR. Two command zero — OBSTACLE_CLEAR ascending in place, ALT_CORRECTION descending in place. Selection runs off the depth error at the vehicle column through a Schmitt band; the cliff-top latch overrides it while committed.

Sequencing lives in mode selection

The two zero-speed modes are the sequencing mechanism: authority to zero one control input for a reason external to that input's own objective. Where relief outruns the depth rate the vehicle halts, converges, then advances.

The criterion is a speed ratio. A vehicle tracking terrain of gradient g at forward speed vx must change depth at g·vx, so the band can be held while surging only where g ≤ vz/vx — 1.0, or 45°, at the deployed speeds. Beyond it the vehicle leaves the band whatever the tuning, and continuing forward simply converts transect into unsurveyed line. Halting instead recovers exactly (Δz / vz) · vx metres of in-band line per correction. Over the 70° sawtooth used in evaluation, an 18 m descent taken under way would cost 18 m of line against a 17.28 m tooth period — more than an entire tooth lost per descent.

Commitment lives in the cliff-top latch

State carried across cycles, with a termination condition independent of the observation. On detecting a forward obstacle the latch commits to a target depth and a peak position; the target ratchets shallower only, the peak forward only; release is position-gated at peak plus standoff plus vehicle length, or on a heading change beyond the staleness gate. While committed the commanded depth is the latch target and any mode other than OBSTACLE_CLEAR is overridden to OBSTACLE_HOLD, so the vehicle either climbs to the target or flies forward at it, and never descends mid-traverse.

The release condition cannot be expressed as a duration. It is a distance, the required distance is not known at commit time because the peak ratchets forward as shallower terrain enters the window, and — decisively — sequencing zeroes forward speed, so elapsed time and distance travelled decouple. The two mechanisms are not merely independent; a time-based commitment is incompatible with a sequencing controller by construction.

The two live in different variables — latch state and mode — and neither reads the other.

The vehicle-column command

Takes the shallower of the hull-footprint manifold minimum and the latest direct vertical range from DVL or altimeter. On a slope the nadir column knows nothing about terrain the bow and stern sit over; and after a turn wipes the map the manifold can be optimistic while a current altitude return is not. A stationary vehicle post-turn on a 70° face was commanded to descend from a manifold reading 1.08 m deeper than the true terrain under its stern, against a 1.0 m tail-safety band that consequently never engaged.

Sensor validity comes from the sensor

Beam validity is taken from what the message reports — per-beam flags and documented sentinels — not from a configured maximum range. A real sensor has already applied its own limit, so clamping a valid return to a configured maximum invents terrain: a genuine 30 m bottom lock reported as a hit at 25 m is a fabricated obstacle. The forward sonar is the exception, and structurally so: its message carries no validity field.

Representation validity under rotation

Voxels carry the heading at which they were last observed and are discarded when the vehicle turns beyond 45°, with occupied voxels under the hull footprint exempt. Not a tuning knob but an explicit model of when the 2-D projection is valid, and the reason a turn is the hardest moment for this controller.

05

The ideal trajectory, and the measured gap

Because the objective is in-band line over a fixed transect, the ideal is characterisable rather than merely computable, and the characterisation is short.

Any metre flown while outside the band is a metre of transect not surveyed. So forward motion out of band is only ever justified when it is required — when the vehicle must cross a feature before it may descend, the tail-clearance case. Everywhere else, motion out of band can be deferred at no cost, because the terrain is not going anywhere and the vehicle can correct in place first.

Surge if and only if the vehicle is in band, or forward motion is required to clear a feature before it may descend. Correct in place otherwise.

That is the deployed mode structure. ALT_FOLLOW surges in band; the two zero-speed modes hold station out of band; OBSTACLE_HOLD and TAIL_CLEAR surge out of band only while clearing. The controller is not approximating a policy discovered elsewhere — the policy structure falls out of the objective.

The ideal is analytic per terrain profile. In-band line equals the transect length, less three compulsory intervals: faces whose gradient exceeds vz/vx, the standoff preceding each rising face, and the half-length plus standoff after each descending edge before the stern has crossed. No numerical optimisation over the mesh is needed to state the ceiling; it follows directly from the profile, for synthetic and reconstructed terrain alike.

What the controller achieves against it

On the evaluation terrain — a four-leg lawnmower flown across 70° sawtooth teeth of 20 m relief, three noise seeds — the ceiling is 46.2 m of a 159.2 m surveyed transect. The other 113.0 m is compulsory: 73.0 m of face too steep to follow, 16.0 m of approach standoff, 24.0 m of tail clearance. The controller holds the band for 31.2 ± 0.7 m, or 67.5% of the ceiling.

The transect is measured from where survey altitude is first reached rather than from launch. The descent from the surface is set by start depth and vertical speed rather than by terrain, and the ideal assumes a vehicle already at altitude, so it is removed from both sides and numerator and denominator span the same ground. This does not flatter the result: that descent runs entirely over a face the ideal already writes off, so the ceiling and the ratio are unchanged by the exclusion.

Compulsoriness is tested by position, not by mode name

A ratio alone cannot say whether the missing 15.0 m was ever available. Reading it off the control modes — matching OBSTACLE_HOLD to unfollowable faces, TAIL_CLEAR to clearance — is tempting and wrong, because the modes do not partition the terrain the way the causes do. Done that way the largest error in the run reads as 0.8 m; measured properly it is 9.2 m. So every metre is classified instead by where it was flown: whether the vehicle's along-leg position fell inside one of the ideal's own compulsory intervals.

The residual gap is not perception

An earlier draft of this section conjectured that whatever the controller loses against the analytic ideal is attributable to acting on a sparse, causal, heading-limited map rather than on the profile itself — that the policy is optimal given knowledge, so the gap measures the cost of sensing. The oracle ablation was run to test exactly that, and it does not survive.

Replacing beam-derived occupancy with occupancy written from the terrain itself, leaving the controller untouched, moves in-band line from 31.2 m to 32.4 m. That is 2.6 percentage points of a 32.5-point shortfall. Avoidable loss falls from 11.5 m to 10.0 m.

The breakdown says why. Of the 11.5 m of avoidable loss, 9.2 m is spent in OBSTACLE_HOLD — and the oracle spends 9.5 m there, marginally more. The dominant recoverable term is untouched by perfect knowledge, because it is not an inference failure: it is the standoff and commitment policy holding out of band longer than the terrain requires. That is a control question, and it is the one worth pursuing.

Perception explains one term cleanly. TAIL_CLEAR contributes 2.0 m of avoidable loss under real beams against 0.4 m under the oracle, which is what a noisy upper-envelope estimate of a descending edge should cost. It is also the smaller half of the story, and the oracle buys it at a worse worst-case clearance — 0.809 m against 0.930 m — because a controller that sees more descends sooner.

A priced safety premium. ALT_FOLLOW spends 6.8 m out of band, and the cause is deliberate. The altitude estimate is the minimum vertical component across the Janus beams, which on sloping ground biases the commanded depth a median 2.9 m shallow and binds on 63% of those metres. The manifold is an along-track projection and has no representation for a hazard abeam, so that overstatement is the only thing standing between the vehicle and a wall it is set sideways onto by current — it is not a duplicate of what the obstacle latch already knows, it is the sole evidence the wall exists. The positional test prices the choice: just 0.3 m of the 6.8 m falls on ground that was followable. The premium is paid almost entirely over terrain that was never surveyable, which is the strongest argument available for keeping it.

Stated carefully, the exchange argument above is a characterisation, not a proof. It assumes the transect is fixed rather than the mission duration, that correcting in place is always available — which is where hover capability enters as a premise rather than a convenience — that the three intervals above are the only compulsory out-of-band motion, and that the vehicle is a point, which costs about 1.2 m of the measured gap on this terrain. The ceiling is also a ceiling for this policy class: a controller free to creep at vz/g could hold the band across a steep face, at a survey rate that would make the imagery useless.

06

Liveness by construction

A property of the deployed implementation, worth stating in its own right. The controller this replaced could livelock — its history carries a fix meant to help the rise-over routine "resolve if stuck" — and the deployed mode graph is small enough to rule that class out by construction.

Every maximal interval of cycles commanding zero forward speed is finite, except one: the vehicle held at the surface by a target it cannot reach. Mission end aside, that is the only state from which forward progress does not resume.

The transition relation was enumerated exhaustively over the mode selection, the latch override and the tail-clearance hook, across the range of depth error, previous mode, latch state and tail state. Zero forward speed occurs in exactly two modes; the branch handling both mode predicates being true is unreachable, so the automaton has three outcomes rather than four; and of the twenty-four edges in the relation, exactly one is absent — the one that matters.

An ascent, once begun, is completed. OBSTACLE_CLEAR exits upward only — to OBSTACLE_HOLD when the latch carries it forward, or to ALT_FOLLOW when the target is reached — and never straight into a descent. While climbing the vehicle has not moved, so nothing that justified the climb has changed except its own altitude; a command to descend at that instant means the slanted beams have begun to see past the obstacle, which is the observation this design exists to refuse mid-manoeuvre. The legitimate form of that transition happens after the vehicle has flown over the feature, from OBSTACLE_HOLD, and is untouched.

The latch enforced this wherever it applied; outside a latch it needed one guard, now in place. With it, the induced subgraph on the two zero-velocity states holds a single edge and is acyclic, so no cycle can live there and every path between them exits through the forward-progress band. The self-transitions carry separate bounds: ALT_CORRECTION descends against a command bounded by the grid floor; OBSTACLE_CLEAR ascends toward a target that is monotone, clamped at zero, and quantised by the grid — so monotone-and-bounded gives finitely many changes rather than asymptotic convergence, which closes the argument in finite time rather than in the limit.

Two honest notes. The guard changed no outcome across the full mission — OBSTACLE_CLEAR exited to OBSTACLE_HOLD thirty-four times and otherwise persisted, and the forbidden transition never arose, because every rise in that terrain lies ahead of the nose so the forward scan fires and the latch commits. It is a safety net for a case the current scenarios do not produce. The geometry that would exercise it is a pinnacle beneath the hull but not ahead of the nose, where the footprint rule drives an ascent without the latch committing; a fixture for that is listed in §8.

And this is a proof about the mode logic, not about the mapping, the sensors, or whether the commanded depth is correct. Safety — that the command never intersects the manifold plus margin — is a separate property over a stated terrain and sensor model, and is not claimed here. Model checking would confirm the case analysis mechanically; the finite-time bounds come from monotonicity, not from search.

Design rationale, and the alternatives

Tuning is thresholds, not weights

Every parameter is a physical margin an operator can state without knowing the control law:

45 °
Grid bin
0.5 × 0.25 m

"Fly two metres above the seabed, stand off two metres from a cliff, never let terrain come within a metre of the hull." Each is independently meaningful and independently adjustable: raising the standoff does not change what the safety band means. That is the difference between commissioning a vehicle by stating physical margins and commissioning it by balancing gains until the behaviour looks right, and it is the kind of difference that decides whether a method survives contact with an operations team.

Why not a task-priority formulation

The obvious alternative is set-based task-priority control, which is the established way to resolve competing objectives in marine robotics. It is worth being straightforward about what it can do here: given the same two mechanisms it solves the same problem. We verified this in simulation — a set-based controller with sequencing and a latched geometric release performs comparably to the deployed design — and the claim is therefore about what the problem requires, not about which formalism may hold it.

What the framework does not supply is the state. A set-based task activates on the current value of its constraint, and a peak that has passed behind the vehicle appears in no constraint value; carrying it means adding a latched flag, a ratcheted reference and set/clear rules above the arbitration — a hybrid automaton by another name. Task priority resolves competing tasks given the state; deciding what the state must be is a separate question, and it is the one this paper answers.

Given that, the choice was made on other grounds. The parameters are physical margins rather than priority weights. The mode set is small enough to carry a liveness argument (§6), which a continuous arbitration does not naturally state. And the structure matches a 2-D manifold built for straight transects, where there is no redundancy for a hierarchy to resolve — the vehicle commands one degree of freedom, and a priority framework's central strength is not exercised.

That calculus changes in 3-D. Lifting the control law to a volumetric representation with lateral avoidance introduces exactly the multi-objective redundancy task priority handles well, and the mode set would need redesign rather than extension. If this work is carried to 3-D, the formulation is the natural thing to revisit, and the ingredients identified here transfer into it unchanged — which is the practical value of stating them independently of the architecture.

Evaluation

The headline measurement is the gap between the realised trajectory and the analytic ideal of §5, on the same terrain. Everything else either explains that gap or establishes that it survives contact with a real stack.

Terrain and stack fidelity are independent choices — synthetic profiles run in Gazebo as readily as a photogrammetric mesh — so the instruments differ by cost per run, which decides what is affordable rather than what is worth doing.

What belongs in the logic simulation, and in what order

All of it before SITL, because every analytic claim in §4 and §5 is cheap to falsify here and expensive to falsify there. A surprise at this stage changes the paper; a surprise after a week of SITL runs changes the paper and wastes the week.

  • run 1 · Gap to the analytic ideal. In-band line computed from the terrain profile and measured against what the controller achieves: 31.2 m of a 46.2 m ceiling, with every metre classified against the ideal's own compulsory intervals by position (§5). What remains is the sweep — tooth angle, amplitude and flat length — to map where the gap opens rather than reporting it at one operating point.
  • run 2 · Oracle-perception ablation. The same controller against a manifold written from the terrain rather than from beams. It answers §5's central conjecture in the negative: in-band line rises only 31.2 → 32.4 m, and the dominant avoidable term is marginally worse under perfect knowledge (9.5 m against 9.2 m). The gap is control, not sensing, which redirects the remaining work onto the standoff and commitment policy.
  • 3 · The gradient criterion. Sweep face angle through the predicted 45° boundary at fixed speeds, and through the corresponding boundary at other speed ratios. Either the followable/unfollowable transition sits where vz/vx says it does, or the analysis in §4 is wrong. Cheap, and it validates an equation the paper leans on.
  • 4 · The straddled-pinnacle fixture. A pinnacle beneath the hull but not ahead of the nose — the one geometry that exercises §6's liveness guard, which is currently untested by execution. Show the unguarded logic oscillating and the guarded logic escaping.
  • 5 · Parameter sensitivity. Vary the physical thresholds one at a time and show behaviour degrades predictably and in the direction the name implies. This is what turns the tuning argument of §7 from an assertion into evidence, and it is only affordable here.
  • 6 · Degraded sensing. DVL dropout, altimeter loss, map wipes at turns, non-finite returns. Easy to inject here and awkward to induce in SITL, and it exercises the validity-gating and conservative-command paths that hardening produced.

Items 1 and 2 are the paper, and both are now run; what they changed is which of the remaining items matters. Item 3 protects an equation the argument rests on. Item 5 is promoted: with the gap shown to be a control property, the standoff and commitment thresholds are where the recoverable 9.2 m lives, so sweeping them is no longer only a tuning-argument exhibit. Items 4 and 6 are each a paragraph in results and a reviewer question pre-empted.

What SITL adds, and why it comes second

Real vehicle dynamics rather than a kinematic model, real timing and message latency, sensor plugins in place of ray casts, and mesh geometry that a height field cannot express — overhangs among it. None of those change the analytic claims; they establish that the claims survive a realistic stack and real reef geometry. Running them before the logic simulation has settled items 1 to 3 would be measuring an unvalidated hypothesis at fifty times the cost per run.

The validation chain

  • Logic simulation against SITL, same synthetic profile. Identical geometry, different stack. Agreement licenses the cheap instrument's sweeps as evidence; disagreement isolates the kinematic model, since terrain is held fixed.
  • SITL against the water. The original controller's own binary, over the reconstruction of the reef it actually surveyed. Because it is the same code, divergence is attributable to the simulator rather than to a reimplementation. Compare distributions over bounded windows, not trajectories — navigation drift guarantees path divergence under identical control.

Chained, those two bridges carry credibility from the water back to the cheap sweeps: the parameter studies are trusted because SITL reproduces them on identical terrain, and SITL is trusted because it reproduces what the vehicle actually did.

Held

  • have A validated C++ core deployed as an LCM node, version-pinned into the vehicle codebase and shared by the logic simulation and the replay tool.
  • have Extensive reef-slope logs, and metrically accurate photogrammetric reconstructions of those sites from the vehicle's own stereo imagery.
  • have The predecessor controller, recoverable from version control — a real comparative baseline that actually flew these missions.
  • have Representation and timing measurements taken from the deployed core, and a seeded, reproducible harness.

What none of it is

No instrument here has this controller commanding the real vehicle over real reef. Say so once, plainly. Sensor plugins approximate acoustics even in SITL; navigation is perfect where the real mission accumulates drift; reconstruction coverage is densest where the earlier controller flew and possibly thin where this one would fly differently; and a dense mesh must be decimated for physics, which removes small-scale relief first — precisely the rugosity the controller responds to, so confirm the collision mesh still carries structure at the vehicle's own length scale.

Limitations to state first

  • The map assumes locally straight transects. The 2-D projection is valid only while heading is roughly constant; the 45° staleness gate is an admission of this and belongs in the scope statement, not as a knob.
  • Single degree of freedom. The controller commands depth — over, never around. This is where a task-priority formulation would genuinely win, absorbing additional DOF where the mode set would need redesign.
  • Hover capability is a premise. Correcting in place is what makes the optimal policy available at all. A vehicle that cannot hold station cannot fly this trajectory, and the ideal for such a platform is a different and harder problem.
  • Overhangs are flown over, not under. Safe, but no imagery beneath one and altitude there is measured from the wrong surface. A coverage bound on the method.
  • One terminal state. Slopes too steep to traverse leave the vehicle at the surface, by design, failing safe rather than commanding through terrain.
  • The ideal is an upper bound on a model. It assumes a fixed transect rather than a fixed mission duration, and treats clearance holds as the only compulsory out-of-band motion. Both should be stated where the ceiling is introduced.
