Publication positioning · draft outline

Sequencing Over Arbitration

Surveying rugose reef terrain at a fixed imaging altitude needs two things at once — commitment through a manoeuvre, and the sequencing of translation against vertical acquisition. A priority hierarchy has a single arbitration channel to express both through, so they compete. Separating them is what the deployed supervisory design buys, and it is measurable. Underneath sits a 2-D manifold representation that makes five sensor rays per cycle enough to plan on over reef terrain.

01

The claim

An earlier version of this outline argued commitment under lost observability as the thesis. The experiments do not support that framing: commitment alone changes almost nothing, and once the planner's vehicle-column command was corrected, no controller collides from losing sight of an obstacle. The evidence supports a different and sharper claim.

Holding a survey altitude over rugose terrain requires temporal sequencing — halting translation until the vertical task has converged, then advancing — and requires commitment through a manoeuvre for safety. Task-priority control can be given either, but expresses both through the same channel: which task is currently active. Holding a set-based task active for commitment directly consumes the time the altitude task needs to converge. The two contend. A supervisory structure separates them, and only the separated design achieves both.

The contribution is therefore not "a state machine for AUV altitude control", which is well-trodden. It is the identification of two requirements that a single priority hierarchy cannot satisfy simultaneously, demonstrated by building the hierarchy, adding each mechanism, and measuring where it fails.

At eight pages three contributions fit, provided they are ordered rather than balanced. The contention result leads. The vehicle-relative 2-D manifold is the second — it is what makes planning possible under the platform's sensing budget at all, and it is cheap enough to run inside the vehicle's own node (§3). The third is the deployed system itself: one version-pinned core flying on the vehicle, driving the simulator, and replaying field logs, with in-water validation on reef terrain.

02

Why the survey is hard

The motivating environment is highly rugose reef terrain — coral reef slopes, where vertical profiles, canyons, overhangs, bommies and pervasive small-scale structural variability sit within the survey corridor. Flying at a fixed imaging altitude puts the vehicle inside that structure rather than safely above it, so terrain is a collision hazard rather than a mapping nuisance, and the hazards are not isolated features to be avoided one at a time but a continuous property of the seabed.

That environment drives everything that follows, through three properties.

Structure is dense and local. Relief varies on scales comparable to the vehicle itself, so the seabed under the hull is not well described by the reading directly beneath it, and commanded depth derived from a single nadir altitude will put one end of the vehicle into terrain the other end never saw.

The obstacle stops being observable mid-manoeuvre. Downward Janus beams and the altimeter cannot see a feature above the vehicle, and the forward sonar's return is lost during a climb. Yet tail clearance requires the manoeuvre to continue past a crest the vehicle can no longer sense.

Altitude cannot be tracked while advancing. Where relief is steep the seabed changes faster than the depth rate can follow, so a vehicle that keeps surging never settles onto the imaging altitude at all.

03

The representation earns its own claim

Before the control argument there is a representation argument, and it stands on its own. A seafloor survey AUV senses the world through five rays per cycle — three Janus beams, an altimeter, a forward sonar. That is far too sparse to populate a volumetric map: at 8 Hz it would take roughly four hours of ray endpoints merely to touch each cell of the equivalent 3-D grid once, by which time the vehicle has long since moved on.

Collapsing occupancy onto the vehicle-relative vertical plane, and reducing that to the upper-envelope polyline the planner actually consumes, is what makes sparse sensing sufficient. Every return lands in the plane the survey is flown in.

Sixty times fewer cells, and the whole planning pipeline — stale-voxel clearing, manifold extraction, commanded-depth profile and waypoints — runs in 21 µs. Against the 5 Hz the vehicle publishes at, that is a duty cycle of 0.01%: the representation is free on the embedded target, which is why it can run inside the LCM node rather than on a companion computer.

And it is dense enough to plan on. Over the survey, in steady state:

Columns without a real observation are flagged rather than guessed at, so the avoidance checks skip them instead of triggering on the grid-floor default — the representation carries its own uncertainty rather than pretending to completeness. The 13.3% figure is the post-turn low, when the heading gate has invalidated the map and it is rebuilding; that recovery window is where the planner's rule of commanding the vehicle column from the most conservative source available — the shallowest manifold across the hull footprint, or the latest direct altitude return if shallower — earns its place.

Overhangs deserve their own sentence, because they are named among the reef hazards and an upper envelope cannot represent them. What it does instead is take the shallowest occupied voxel in each column, so an overhang's underside becomes the manifold and the vehicle is commanded above it: the planner always goes over structure, never under it. That is the conservative failure mode, and for a downward-imaging survey it is arguably the correct one — the cost is coverage beneath the overhang, not safety. Worth claiming as a property rather than conceding as a gap.

The genuine limitation is the projection itself: it is valid only while heading is roughly constant. That belongs in §10, not buried.

04

The experiment

The test terrain is a parameterised proxy for reef rugosity rather than a reef itself: a repeatable, tunable worst case that isolates the control question. A 4-leg lawnmower flown across a 70° sawtooth ridge field — 20 m teeth on a 30 m seafloor with 10 m flats — with legs along the axis the teeth progress on, so each 40 m leg crosses roughly two and a third crests. The vehicle launches at the surface; the descent is excluded. Each leg transition is two 90° on-the-spot yaws, which exceed the stale-heading gate and invalidate the map.

All controllers share the deployed perception stack — the same occupancy grid, the same cliff manifold, the same measurements. Only the arbitration differs, so any difference measured is a property of the control law. Each condition is 15 noise realisations, the same seeds across controllers.

The simulator also carries a procedural reef generator — bommies, terraces, cliff-like faults — which is the more representative scenario and is listed in §8 as outstanding. The sawtooth is what the current numbers come from, and the paper should say so plainly.

The figure of merit is time held at imaging altitude, not ground covered: halting to reach altitude before moving on is correct behaviour for this survey, so scoring coverage would penalise the right answer.

Three readings, in order of how much weight they carry.

Sequencing is what the mission needs. Adding stop-to-converge alone takes time-on-altitude from 1.0% to 6.7% — a sevenfold gain — where commitment alone moves it from 1.0% to 1.1%, which is nothing.

Sequencing alone is unsafe. Halting to converge means descending in place beside steep terrain, and without commitment the vehicle does that into the seabed in four runs of fifteen.

Both together still fall short. Commitment restores safety — no collisions, worst clearance back above zero — but costs a third of the mission gain, ending at 4.9% against the deployed controller's 7.7% (t ≈ 11). And its worst-case clearance is 6 cm, safe only barely.

05

Why they compete

The mechanism is measurable, not inferred. Adding dwell to the sequencing variant shifts where the controller spends its time:

set-based active
34.9 → 53.1 %
altitude converging
58.0 → 41.8 %

Commitment is expressed by holding a set-based task active. Sequencing is expressed by the altitude task running long enough to converge. In a priority hierarchy both are the same variable — which task is active — so every cycle spent committed is a cycle not spent converging. The trade is structural, not a tuning artefact.

The supervisory design does not face it, because the two live in different places: the cliff latch governs the depth target held through an obstacle traverse, while mode selection governs whether the vehicle surges. They compose instead of contending.

This is the paper's core argument and it is falsifiable: if some dwell duration escapes the trade-off, the claim is wrong. See §8.

06

Tuning is thresholds, not weights

A second advantage, and a real one for field deployment. Every parameter of the deployed controller is a physical threshold an operator can state without knowing the control law:

2.0 m

2.0 m

1.0 m

2.0 m
Heading gate
45 °
Grid bin
0.5 × 0.25 m

"Fly two metres above the seabed, stand off two metres from a cliff, never let terrain come within a metre of the hull." Each is independently meaningful and independently adjustable — raising the standoff does not change what the safety band means.

The task-priority variants need a dwell duration and a convergence band whose only interpretation is how much priority one task takes from another, and §5 shows they interact: changing one changes what the other does. That is the difference between commissioning a vehicle by stating physical margins and commissioning it by balancing gains until the behaviour looks right — and it is the kind of difference that decides whether a method survives contact with an operations team.

07

What can and cannot be claimed

Determinism is not a contribution — most classical controllers are deterministic. Verifiability is, and the design is well placed for it: a small finite mode set, an explicit priority order, transitions on scalar comparisons.

Claimable, with work

  • Safety. Commanded depth never intersects the manifold plus margin, over a stated terrain class and sensor model.
  • Termination. The latch releases in finite time under forward progress — monotone ratchets make release_x non-decreasing while along-track position strictly increases.
  • No chatter. The hysteresis band on mode selection admits a dwell-time argument.

Not claimable as stated

"The vehicle can never enter a stuck control state" is too strong, and the source documents the exception: where the obstacle top is above the surface, the vehicle ascends against its depth limit and stays there. Presented as a single designed terminal condition — reached only when the task is infeasible, failing safe rather than commanding through terrain, with escape defined at mission level — it is a strength. Presented as an absolute, a reviewer finds the exception and distrusts the rest.

Name the artifact

A correctness property holds of something specific: a model, or the code that flies. State the property over the model of §III, establish it there, and say how the implementation is held to it. That link is easy here — the deployed core is one version-pinned artifact shared by the vehicle node, the simulator and the replay path, so the thing tested is the thing that flies.

08

Evidence: held and outstanding

In hand

  • have The comparison above: five controllers, shared perception, 15 seeded noise realisations, reproducible from the repository.
  • have A set-based task-priority baseline with commitment and sequencing available independently — the thing reviewers will ask for.
  • have A validated C++ core deployed as an LCM node, version-pinned into the vehicle codebase.
  • have Field logs and a replay tool driving the identical mapper the vehicle runs, with the same sensor gating on both paths.
  • have Representation measurements — cell count, memory, 21 µs pipeline timing, observed-column fraction — taken from the deployed core rather than estimated.

Outstanding

  • do Sweep the dwell duration. §5 predicts no setting escapes the trade-off. If one does, the necessity claim is wrong and the paper changes. This is the single most important remaining experiment.
  • do Formal model and check for the safety and termination properties.
  • do Decide whether the Python simulator also needs the mesh. If the overhang claim is to be tested there rather than in SITL, its sensors need ray–triangle intersection rather than the current height-field test — see below. If SITL carries that claim instead, the Python instrument can stay on parameterised terrain, which is what it is good at.
  • do Run both controllers in Gazebo SITL over the reef reconstruction. Headline closed-loop results, with both as their real implementations. The original is recoverable from version control at the commit before oa-mapper replaced it, and doubles as the simulator-fidelity arm.
  • do Reconstruction coverage check. Confirm the mesh is dense where the counterfactual trajectories would fly, not only where the earlier controller went.
  • do A cliff regression case that validates rather than detects. The current one is degenerate: past the step the vehicle sits below the surface and the caster hits on every beam.

Three instruments, and what each is for

The field data is extensive and from genuinely complex reef slopes, but the vehicle flew a different and much simpler avoidance method at the time. That constrains what each instrument can support, and the constraints should be stated before a reviewer states them.

  • A — Gazebo SITL over the reef reconstruction. to run The full AUV stack with the photogrammetric mesh loaded: real nodes, real LCM traffic, real vehicle dynamics, real timing. Both controllers run as their own implementations rather than ports, which removes the "did you reimplement the baseline faithfully" objection entirely. This is the primary closed-loop evidence and the headline results should come from here.
  • B — the Python simulator on parameterised terrain. have Cheap enough for what A cannot afford: fifteen noise realisations across five controllers, dwell and horizon sweeps, ablations that isolate one mechanism at a time. Synthetic terrain is a feature here, not a compromise — it is the only way to hold everything else fixed. This is where the contention result is established; A is where it is confirmed.
  • C — open-loop replay on recorded sensor streams. have The algorithm consuming real DVL, altimeter and sonar returns along the real trajectory. The only instrument that exercises real sensing with real failure modes — dropouts, multipath, beam-quality variation — none of which A or B produce. Supports per-cycle counterfactuals against the method that actually flew.

State the division of labour explicitly in the paper. A reviewer who sees synthetic-terrain sweeps and full-stack runs on real bathymetry and replay on real returns will read it as a considered experimental design; the same three presented without the rationale reads as three attempts at the same thing.

Fly the original controller in SITL — it validates the instrument

The controller that flew these missions is recoverable in full from version control, immediately prior to its replacement. It is a reactive altitude-following method: take the smaller of the sonar and nav altitudes, halt when too close, ramp forward velocity down on altitude error, with a rise-over routine that accrued a later fix described as helping it "resolve if stuck".

Running it in SITL is worth having as a comparison arm — a baseline that genuinely flew, from before this work existed, is far harder to dismiss than one built for the paper. But that is the lesser reason.

The greater reason is that it validates the instrument. The same binary flew the real mission over this reef and can fly it again over its reconstruction. Comparing the two answers the question every reviewer has about simulation on real geometry — does it reproduce reality? — with a measurement rather than an assurance. And because it is the same code, any divergence is attributable to the simulation itself, not to a reimplementation. Establish fidelity once with the old controller and every other arm inherits it.

Design it as distributions rather than trajectories: nav drift and disturbances guarantee divergence over a full mission even under identical control, so compare mode occupancy, altitude and clearance histograms over bounded windows. Treat a negative result as a result — if SITL does not reproduce the old controller's behaviour, that is worth knowing before the paper rests on it, and it points at the sensor or dynamics model rather than at the terrain.

What none of the three is

  • Not a closed-loop field trial. No instrument here has this controller commanding the real vehicle over real reef. Say so once, plainly, and the rest of the evaluation reads as honest rather than as an omission.
  • Simulated acoustics, even in SITL. Sensor plugins approximate the returns; multipath, dropout structure and beam-quality variation belong to instrument C alone. The gating logic this system depends on is exercised properly only there.
  • Reconstruction coverage is not neutral. The mesh comes from imagery the vehicle collected while flying the earlier controller, so it is densest where that controller went — and potentially thin exactly where this one would fly differently, such as closer over crests the old method stood off from. Check coverage against the counterfactual trajectories rather than assuming it.
  • Watch the collision mesh. A dense photogrammetric reconstruction has to be decimated for physics, and decimation removes small-scale relief first — which is precisely the rugosity the controller is responding to. Confirm the decimated mesh still carries structure at the vehicle's own length scale, or the simulation will quietly flatter every controller equally.

The weak method that actually flew is an asset rather than an embarrassment: it is a real-world baseline with a real outcome. Where it lost altitude or closed on terrain, the per-cycle counterfactual shows what this controller would have commanded at that same state — which is exactly the defensible form of the claim, and more persuasive on real reef slopes than any synthetic scenario.

Disclose

  • gap The altimeter no-return test compares range against a configured maximum, standing in for the quality field and zero sentinel the message already carries.
  • gap Off-slant DVL beams may fill but not clear voxels — their 2-D projection misrepresents which voxels they traversed.
  • gap The forward sonar message carries no validity flag at all, so its no-return inference is necessarily heuristic.

Proposed structure

Eight pages — the six-page RA-L base plus the two over-length pages — with the conference option for the talk. That is roughly two pages more than the minimum cut, and the right place to spend them is the representation and the results, not a longer introduction. Verify the current page and fee rules against the call; they move.

  1. I

    Introduction · ~1 pp

    Reef-slope survey at fixed imaging altitude; rugosity as a continuous hazard rather than isolated obstacles. Contributions as three ordered bullets, contention first.

  2. II

    Related work · ~0.75 pp

    Task-priority and null-space control in marine robotics, set-based tasks, reactive avoidance, underwater occupancy and elevation mapping. Position against arbitration, not against mapping.

  3. III

    Representation: the cliff manifold · ~1.25 pp

    Its own section at this length. The sensing budget makes a volumetric map unfillable; the projection is what makes five rays per cycle sufficient. Cell count, memory, 21 µs pipeline, observed-column fraction, observed-versus-inferred flagging, and overhangs traversed above rather than under.

  4. IV

    Supervisory controller · ~1.25 pp

    Mode set, priority order, the latch with its monotone ratchets and release conditions, and the vehicle-column rule taking the most conservative available source. Present as a hybrid automaton, with the separation of commitment from sequencing made explicit — this is what the results then test.

  5. V

    Task-priority baseline · ~0.75 pp

    Construction, the reduction to strict priority in one DOF, and the two additions as independent switches. Short but load-bearing: it is what makes the comparison credible rather than a straw man.

  6. VI

    Experiments and results · ~2.5 pp

    Mission and metric definition, the five-controller table, the contention mechanism, the dwell sweep, and field results on reef terrain. Headline: sequencing carries the mission, commitment carries safety, one channel cannot carry both.

  7. VII

    Discussion · ~0.5 pp

    Threshold tuning versus priority weighting; the terrain-imposed ceiling; limitations stated plainly.

  8. VIII

    Conclusion · ~0.25 pp

Figure budget

Six figures and one table is about right for eight pages. Every one should carry an argument rather than illustrate the system.

  • 1 · The environment. Reef-slope bathymetry with the vehicle at imaging altitude inside the structure. Establishes that the hazard is continuous.
  • 2 · Representation. Occupancy grid to manifold to commanded-depth profile, one panel each, with the unobserved columns visibly flagged.
  • 3 · The manoeuvre. The depth-profile figure: commit, the unobservable span, release after tail clearance.
  • 4 · Mode structure. The hybrid automaton, with the commitment and sequencing paths drawn as separate channels — this figure is the argument.
  • Table I · Results. The controllers — task priority and its two additions, the original deployed method, and this one — with commitment and sequencing columns, time-on-altitude, clearance and collisions.
  • 5 · Contention. Set-based occupancy against altitude convergence as dwell increases; the dwell sweep on the same axes. The mechanism made visible.
  • 6 · Real reef slope. A recorded transect: reconstructed bathymetry, the mode trace this controller produces on the logged sensor stream, and the depth the earlier method actually flew alongside what this one would have commanded. Label the counterfactual as such in the caption.

What still does not fit

  • Formal verification of safety and termination. Cite as future work; it deserves its own treatment.
  • The full deployment argument. The version-pinned artifact and the vehicle/simulator/replay sharing is a paragraph in Discussion, not a section — however much it is worth in practice.

Use the video

RA-L takes a supplementary video and reviewers watch them. The repository already has a browser visualizer that renders the grid, manifold, commanded-depth profile and mode against terrain in real time. A side-by-side of the deployed controller and the task-priority baseline crossing the same crest would carry the contention argument in fifteen seconds better than any figure — and it costs a screen recording, not new work.

Limitations to state first

  • The map assumes locally straight transects. Collapsing 3-D structure into a vehicle-relative vertical slice is valid only while heading is roughly constant; the 45° staleness gate is an admission of this and should be presented as a scope condition of the representation, not a tuning knob.
  • Single degree of freedom. The controller commands depth. It goes over, never around — and this is where task priority genuinely wins, since it absorbs additional DOF where the mode set would need redesign.
  • Overhangs are flown over, not under. The upper envelope cannot represent them, and treats the underside as the surface. Safe, but no imagery is acquired beneath an overhang and altitude there is measured from the wrong surface. State it as a coverage bound on the method, since reef slopes genuinely contain them.
  • One terminal state. Slopes too steep to traverse, as in §7.
  • The test terrain is near the edge of feasibility. A 70° 20 m sawtooth permits at most ~19% time-on-altitude at 0.5 m/s, since each tooth costs about 72 s of vertical transit against 35 s of along-track. The deployed controller reaches ~40% of that ceiling. Report the ceiling alongside the result, or the absolute numbers will read as failure.

Two rows must move before submission. The dwell sweep is not optional: without it the contention claim is unbounded and a reviewer will say so in one line. And the reef data must be converted from recorded logs into closed-loop results on reconstructed bathymetry — which is an analysis pass on data already held, not a new campaign. Everything else in the table is either done or explicitly deferred.

Eight pages is enough for three ordered contributions — contention, representation, deployed system evaluated on real reef terrain — but only if the third is real. Closed-loop missions over the photogrammetric reef mesh, plus open-loop mode traces on the recorded sensor streams, is what separates this from a simulation paper. Name both for what they are: neither is a closed-loop field trial, and the paper is stronger for saying so than for being caught not saying it.

This closes the JOE path for this work. RA-L is itself a journal, so a later JOE paper on the same material would be a journal-to-journal extension rather than the usual conference-to-journal route, and would need substantial genuinely new content. The conference option supplies the talk without spending a second venue.

If the work grows past eight pages rather than fitting, the clean split is by subject and not by increment: the contention result stays at RA-L, and the representation plus an extended field campaign becomes a separate marine-venue paper on surveying rugose reef terrain under sparse sensing. Two papers that do not overlap, rather than one published twice.
