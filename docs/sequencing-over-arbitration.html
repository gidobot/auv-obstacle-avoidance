Publication positioning · rewritten against the current implementation

Sequencing Over Arbitration

The seafloor defines the ideal survey trajectory: track the terrain profile inside a tight altitude band for as much of the transect as clearance and vehicle limits allow. Reactive altitude following gets most of the way; it cannot climb obstacles, commit to cliffs before their tops are visible, or hold depth until the tail clears an edge. This is a controller for those exceptions — and it flies very nearly the trajectory the terrain permits.

01

The claim

An earlier version of this document argued commitment under lost observability as the thesis. The experiments did not support it: commitment alone moves the mission metric by a tenth of a percentage point, and once the planner's vehicle-column rule was corrected, nothing collides from losing sight of an obstacle. What the work does support is narrower, sharper, and has two halves that share one cause.

Given the manifold, the seafloor defines an ideal trajectory: follow the terrain profile within a tight band of the survey altitude, for as much of the transect as the vehicle's speed and clearance limits allow. The contribution is a controller that realises very nearly that trajectory online, from sparse causal observations, on terrain rugose enough that a reactive follower alone cannot.

A well-tuned reactive altitude follower already tracks smoothly varying terrain well under limited observability — that part is not novel and should not be claimed. What it cannot do is handle the exceptions: obstacles that must be climbed rather than followed, cliffs that must be committed to before their tops are visible, and tails that must clear an edge before the vehicle may descend. The deployed controller is reactive altitude following plus a disciplined set of exceptions — vertical corrections taken at zero forward speed, and geometric latches for clearance — so that the portion of the transect flown inside the band is maximised against the limits the seafloor imposes.

That framing sets the benchmark where it belongs. The question is not whether this beats another controller but how close it comes to the trajectory the terrain permits, which makes the terrain itself the standard and the comparison a supporting result rather than the thesis.

Two ingredients make the exceptions work, and they are separable and measurable. Sequencing — halting translation until the vertical task converges — carries the mission: without it, 1.0% of the transect is in band. Commitment discharged by geometry — a manoeuvre held until the vehicle has passed the feature rather than until a timer expires or the observation lapses — carries safety: without it, sequencing alone drives the vehicle into the seabed in four runs of fifteen. A duration cannot substitute for the second at any setting.

Both transfer. Given the same two ingredients a standard set-based task-priority controller reproduces the deployed design and marginally exceeds it (§5), which is worth reporting plainly: the claim is about what the problem requires, not about which formalism may hold it. What the priority framework does not supply is the state a geometric discharge condition needs — a latched reference that has stopped being observable — and identifying that state is the transferable part.

Scope, stated up front. This is a controller for one style of mission: line-survey transects flown by a hover-capable vehicle over reef slopes, with a 2-D manifold that is valid while heading is roughly constant. The hover capability is what makes zero-speed vertical correction available at all, and the straight-line assumption is what makes the projection sound. Raising the control law to a full 3-D representation would be a different design and quite possibly a different answer; that is not a limitation buried in the last section but a boundary on the claim.

The two terms, defined

The claim is only meaningful if both are defined without reference to the mechanism that supplies them. Each is a way of departing from "act on what is best right now", and they depart differently.

Commitment — a decision persists after the observation that justified it is gone. The control law depends on internal state not recoverable from the current measurement, and the manoeuvre ends on an independent progress criterion rather than on the observation lapsing. It answers keep going.

Sequencing — one control channel is suspended until another has converged. A degree of freedom is driven to zero not because its own objective is met but because a different objective has not yet been reached. It answers not yet.

The distinction from arbitration is what the paper turns on. Arbitration — the device a priority hierarchy provides — answers which task acts this instant. It has no way to say keep going except by holding a task active past its constraint, and no way to say not yet except by holding a task active while another converges. Both, therefore, must be encoded in the same variable: task activation. That is why they contend, and it is a property of the formalism rather than of any particular tuning.

It is also why the fourth cell matters: task priority given both is the strongest form of the objection that the supervisory structure is unnecessary.

Ordered for eight pages: contention leads, liveness follows as the guarantee it unlocks, the representation is method, the deployment is evaluation.

02

The environment

Coral reef slopes, where vertical profiles, canyons, overhangs, bommies and pervasive small-scale variability sit inside the survey corridor. Imaging from a fixed altitude puts the vehicle within that structure rather than safely above it, so terrain is a collision hazard rather than a mapping nuisance — and the hazards are a continuous property of the seabed, not isolated features to be routed around one at a time.

Three consequences drive everything that follows.

Structure is dense and local. Relief varies on scales comparable to the vehicle, so the seabed beneath the hull is not described by the reading directly under it. A command derived from a single nadir altitude puts one end of the vehicle into terrain the other end never saw.

The obstacle stops being observable mid-manoeuvre. Downward Janus beams and the altimeter cannot see a feature above the vehicle, and the forward sonar's return is lost during a climb — yet tail clearance requires the manoeuvre to continue past a crest the vehicle can no longer sense.

Altitude cannot be tracked while advancing. Where relief is steep the seabed changes faster than the depth rate can follow, so a vehicle that keeps surging never settles onto the imaging altitude at all.

03

The representation makes sparse sensing sufficient

The platform senses through five rays per cycle — three Janus beams, an altimeter, a forward sonar. That is far too sparse to fill a volume: at 8 Hz it would take roughly four hours of ray endpoints merely to touch each cell of the equivalent 3-D grid once, by which time the vehicle is long gone. Collapsing occupancy onto the vehicle-relative vertical plane, and reducing that to the upper-envelope polyline the planner consumes, is what makes the sensing budget sufficient. Every return lands in the plane the survey is flown in.

Sixty times fewer cells, and the whole planning pipeline — stale-voxel clearing, manifold extraction, commanded-depth profile, waypoints — runs in 6.4 µs median on a fully observed map. Against the 5 Hz the vehicle publishes at that is a duty cycle of 0.003%: the representation is free on the embedded target, which is why it runs inside the LCM node rather than on a companion computer.

It is also dense enough to plan on. In steady state 78% of columns carry a real observation and 94% ahead of the nose, falling to 13% immediately after a turn has invalidated the map and it is rebuilding. Columns without an observation are flagged rather than guessed at, so the avoidance checks skip them instead of triggering on the grid-floor default — the representation carries its own uncertainty rather than pretending to completeness.

Overhangs are a property, not a gap. An upper envelope cannot represent one, and what it does instead is take the shallowest occupied voxel per column, so an overhang's underside becomes the manifold and the vehicle is commanded above it. The planner goes over structure, never under. For downward imaging that is arguably correct; the cost is coverage beneath the overhang, not safety. The genuine limitation is the projection itself, valid only while heading is roughly constant — §10.

04

The controller as it now stands

Worth stating plainly, because several of the decisions below were arrived at during hardening and are load-bearing for the claims.

Five modes. Three command survey speed — ALT_FOLLOW, OBSTACLE_HOLD, TAIL_CLEAR. Two command zero — OBSTACLE_CLEAR ascending in place, ALT_CORRECTION descending in place. Selection runs off the depth error at the vehicle column through a Schmitt band, and the cliff-top latch overrides it while committed.

Commitment (§1) lives in the cliff-top latch

State carried across cycles, with a termination condition independent of the observation — the two things the definition requires. On detecting a forward obstacle the latch commits to a target depth and a peak position. The target ratchets shallower only; the peak ratchets forward only; release is position-gated at peak plus standoff plus vehicle length, or on a heading change beyond the staleness gate. While committed the commanded depth is the latch target and any mode other than OBSTACLE_CLEAR is overridden to OBSTACLE_HOLD — so the vehicle either climbs to the target or flies forward at it, and never descends mid-traverse.

Sequencing (§1) lives in mode selection

Authority to zero one control input for a reason external to that input's own objective — here, surge held at zero while the vertical error closes. Where relief outruns the depth rate the vehicle halts, converges, then advances, which is what produces time on altitude.

The two mechanisms are in different variables: latch state and mode. Neither reads the other, and that is the whole of the structural claim — §5 measures what happens when a formalism has only one variable to put both in.

The vehicle-column command

Takes the shallower of the hull-footprint manifold minimum and the latest direct vertical range from DVL or altimeter. Two reasons, both learned the hard way. On a slope the nadir column knows nothing about terrain the bow and stern sit over. And after a turn wipes the map the manifold can be optimistic while a current altitude return is not — a stationary vehicle post-turn on a 70° face was commanded to descend from a manifold reading 1.08 m deeper than the true terrain under its stern, against a 1.0 m tail-safety band that consequently never engaged.

Sensor validity comes from the sensor

Beam validity is taken from what the message reports — per-beam flags and documented sentinels — not from a configured maximum range. A real sensor has already applied its own limit, so clamping a valid return to a configured maximum invents terrain: a genuine 30 m bottom lock reported as a hit at 25 m is a fabricated obstacle. The forward sonar is the exception and the reason is structural: its message carries no validity field at all.

Representation validity under rotation

Voxels carry the heading at which they were last observed and are discarded when the vehicle turns beyond 45°, with occupied voxels under the hull footprint exempt. This is not a tuning knob but an explicit model of when the 2-D projection is valid, and it is why a turn is the hardest moment for this controller.

05

What the controller requires

A 4-leg lawnmower across a 70° sawtooth ridge field — 20 m teeth on a 30 m seafloor with 10 m flats, legs along the axis the teeth progress on, so each 40 m leg crosses about two and a third crests. The vehicle launches at the surface; the descent is excluded. Each leg transition is two 90° on-the-spot yaws, which exceed the staleness gate and invalidate the map. The terrain is a parameterised proxy for rugosity, not a reef.

All controllers share the deployed perception stack — same grid, same manifold, same measurements — so only the arbitration differs and any difference is a property of the control law. Fifteen noise realisations per condition, same seeds across controllers. The figure of merit is time held at imaging altitude, not ground covered: halting to reach altitude before advancing is correct behaviour here, so scoring coverage would penalise the right answer.

Sequencing carries the mission. Stop-to-converge alone takes time-on-altitude from 1.0% to 6.7%, a sevenfold gain, where commitment alone moves it to 1.1%.

Sequencing alone is unsafe. Halting to converge means descending in place beside steep terrain, and without commitment the vehicle does that into the seabed in four runs of fifteen.

Both together still fall short — 4.9% against 7.7%, and a worst-case clearance of 6 cm. And the mechanism is measurable, not inferred: adding dwell to the sequencing variant lifts set-based task occupancy from 34.9% to 53.1% while altitude convergence falls from 58.0% to 41.8%. Commitment is expressed by holding a task active; sequencing by the altitude task running long enough to converge; in a hierarchy those are the same variable, so every cycle spent committed is a cycle not spent converging. The trade is structural, not a tuning artefact.

The supervisory design does not face it because the two live in different places — the latch governs the depth target held through a traverse, mode selection governs whether the vehicle surges. They compose instead of contending.

No setting reaches the deployed controller on both axes, so the claim stands. But the result is richer than the monotone trade-off predicted, and the paper is better for the difference.

At two seconds a hierarchy essentially matches the deployed controller on mission quality — 7.6 ± 0.8 against 7.7 ± 0.4, indistinguishable — while still breaching half a metre on twenty-seven cycles against zero. The only collision-free setting, eight seconds, costs 36% of the mission. So the honest form of the claim is not that task priority is poor at this survey. It is that no single duration is simultaneously short enough to stay out of the way and long enough to be safe.

Beyond twelve seconds the frontier does not merely trade — it collapses, to −2.6 m worst clearance and hundreds of breach cycles. The mechanism is measurable: at sixteen seconds the safety task's occupancy more than doubles, from 7.8% to 17.4%, and the worst clearance occurs inside it. The vehicle is held on an activation justified sixteen seconds earlier and driven into terrain by a commitment it should long since have discharged.

That is the sharper claim the sweep bought. Duration is the wrong shape for commitment, because a clock carries no discharge condition. Too short and the manoeuvre is abandoned; too long and the controller acts on a target the world has already invalidated. What a commitment needs is a condition referring to the vehicle's progress past the feature — the deployed latch releases at peak plus standoff plus vehicle length — and that is a statement about geometry, not about elapsed time.

Geometric commitment, inside the hierarchy

Which raises the obvious question, and it deserved an experiment rather than an argument: can a geometric discharge condition be expressed in a task-priority framework? It can. The final row of the table is a set-based controller whose forward task commits to a detected peak, ratchets its target shallower and its peak forward, and releases on position — the deployed latch's discharge condition, with the rest of the priority resolution untouched. It reaches 8.1 ± 0.2 against the deployed 7.7 ± 0.4, with a better worst-case clearance and no breaches: it matches the supervisory design and marginally exceeds it.

So the honest conclusion is not that a hierarchy cannot hold both. It is that the two ingredients are what matter, and they transfer. What the framework does not supply is the auxiliary state a geometric condition requires — a set-based task activates on the current value of its constraint, and a peak that has passed behind the vehicle is no longer in any constraint value. Carrying it means adding a latched flag, a ratcheted reference and set/clear rules above the arbitration. Task priority resolves competing tasks given the state; deciding what the state must be is a separate question, and answering it is the contribution.

Two caveats on reading that row. The variant shares the deployed perception stack entirely and inherits every correction made to it, so the comparison isolates the control law and nothing else. And it has never flown — the deployed controller has, which is why it remains the existence proof and the field-validation platform.

Two cautions on reading the table. Collision counts of one in fifteen are weak evidence against zero; the breach counts, being many events per run rather than one rare one, are the sounder safety discriminator, and they separate cleanly (27 and 14 against 0). And this sweeps one of the baseline's two hand-set parameters — the convergence band is still fixed, and a reviewer may reasonably ask whether it moves the frontier.

06

Liveness by construction

A property of the deployed implementation, worth stating in its own right rather than as evidence against any alternative. The controller this replaced could livelock — its history carries a fix meant to help the rise-over routine "resolve if stuck" — and the deployed mode graph is small enough to rule that class out by construction.

Every maximal interval of cycles commanding zero forward speed is finite, except one: the vehicle held at the surface by a target it cannot reach. Mission end aside, that is the only state from which forward progress does not resume.

The transition relation was enumerated exhaustively over the mode selection, the latch override and the tail-clearance hook, across the range of depth error, previous mode, latch state and tail state. Three facts fall out. Zero forward speed occurs in exactly two modes. The branch handling both mode predicates being true is unreachable, so the automaton has three outcomes rather than four. And of the twenty-four edges in the relation, exactly one is absent — the one that matters.

An ascent, once begun, is completed. OBSTACLE_CLEAR exits upward only — to OBSTACLE_HOLD when the latch carries it forward, or to ALT_FOLLOW when the target is reached — and never straight into a descent. While climbing the vehicle has not moved, so nothing that justified the climb has changed except its own altitude; a command to descend at that instant means the slanted beams have begun to see past the obstacle, which is the observation this design exists to refuse mid-manoeuvre. The legitimate form of that transition happens after the vehicle has flown over the feature, from OBSTACLE_HOLD, and is untouched.

The latch enforced this wherever it applied. Outside a latch it needed one guard, now in place. With it, the induced subgraph on the two zero-velocity states holds a single edge and is acyclic, so no cycle can live there and every path between them exits through the forward-progress band. The self-transitions carry separate bounds: ALT_CORRECTION descends against a command bounded by the grid floor; OBSTACLE_CLEAR ascends toward a target that is monotone, clamped at zero, and quantised by the grid — so monotone-and-bounded gives finitely many changes rather than asymptotic convergence, which closes the argument in finite time rather than in the limit.

Two honest notes. The guard changed no outcome across the full mission — OBSTACLE_CLEAR exited to OBSTACLE_HOLD thirty-four times and otherwise persisted, and the forbidden transition never arose, because every rise in that terrain lies ahead of the nose so the forward scan fires and the latch commits. It is a safety net for a case the current scenarios do not produce, and saying so is better than implying it was load-bearing. The geometry that would exercise it is a pinnacle beneath the hull but not ahead of the nose, where the footprint rule drives an ascent without the latch committing; a fixture for that is outstanding.

And this is a proof about the mode logic, not about the mapping, the sensors, or whether the commanded depth is correct. Safety — that the command never intersects the manifold plus margin — is a separate property over a stated terrain and sensor model, and is not claimed here. Model checking would confirm the case analysis mechanically; the finite-time bounds come from monotonicity, not from search.

A deployment property, and a consequence of the same separation. Every parameter is a physical margin an operator can state without knowing the control law:

"Fly two metres above the seabed, stand off two metres from a cliff, never let terrain come within a metre of the hull." Each is independently meaningful and independently adjustable: raising the standoff does not change what the safety band means.

The task-priority variants need a dwell duration and a convergence band whose only interpretation is how much priority one task takes from another — and §5 measured them interacting. That is the difference between commissioning a vehicle by stating physical margins and commissioning it by balancing gains until the behaviour looks right, and it is the kind of difference that decides whether a method survives contact with an operations team.

The validation chain

Read as a chain rather than as four experiments, the design answers the standing objection to every cell.

  • Python sawtooth against SITL sawtooth. Identical geometry, different stack. Agreement licenses the cheap instrument's sweeps as evidence — which matters, because fifteen seeds across five controllers with parameter sweeps is affordable nowhere else. Disagreement isolates the kinematic model, since terrain is held fixed.
  • SITL reef against the real mission. The original controller's own binary, over the reconstruction of the reef it actually surveyed. Because it is the same code, divergence is attributable to the simulator rather than to a reimplementation. Compare distributions over bounded windows, not trajectories — nav drift guarantees path divergence under identical control.

Chained, those two bridges carry credibility from the water back to the cheap sweeps. The parameter studies are trusted because SITL reproduces them on identical terrain, and SITL is trusted because it reproduces what the vehicle actually did. Two comparisons, no new apparatus, and a stronger position than any single instrument can hold.

Held

  • have The five-controller comparison: shared perception, fifteen seeded realisations, reproducible from the repository.
  • have A set-based task-priority baseline with commitment and sequencing as independent switches — the thing reviewers ask for.
  • have A validated C++ core deployed as an LCM node, version-pinned into the vehicle codebase and shared by simulator and replay.
  • have Extensive reef-slope logs, and metrically accurate photogrammetric reconstructions of those sites from the vehicle's own stereo imagery.
  • have Representation and timing measurements taken from the deployed core.

Outstanding

  • done The dwell sweep — §5. No setting reaches the deployed controller on both axes; the frontier is non-monotone and collapses past twelve seconds.
  • do Compute the offline-optimal trajectory and measure the gap. With full terrain knowledge from the mesh, maximising time in band subject to vertical speed, forward speed and clearance is a well-posed optimisation, and the online controller can then be scored against the trajectory the terrain permits rather than against another controller. This is the headline evaluation the framing implies, and it turns "near-ideal" into a number.
  • do More seeds at 2, 4 and 8 s. One collision in fifteen is thin evidence against zero, and those three points carry the dwell result.
  • do Sweep the convergence band — the baseline's other hand-set parameter, and the obvious next question once dwell is bounded.
  • do Both controllers in SITL over the reef mesh, as their own implementations. Headline results and the fidelity arm at once.
  • do The sawtooth in SITL — cheap from the same parametric definition, and what licenses the sweeps.
  • do A straddled-pinnacle fixture — §6. The one geometry that exercises the liveness guard, currently untested by execution.
  • do Reconstruction coverage check. The mesh is densest where the earlier controller flew, and possibly thin where this one would fly differently.

What none of it is

No instrument here has this controller commanding the real vehicle over real reef. Say so once, plainly. Sensor plugins approximate acoustics even in SITL; navigation is perfect where the real mission accumulates drift; and a dense reconstruction must be decimated for physics, which removes small-scale relief first — precisely the rugosity the controller responds to, so confirm the collision mesh still carries structure at the vehicle's own length scale or the simulation will flatter every controller equally.

Limitations to state first

  • The map assumes locally straight transects. The 2-D projection is valid only while heading is roughly constant; the 45° staleness gate is an admission of this and should be presented as a scope condition of the representation, not a knob.
  • Single degree of freedom. The controller commands depth — over, never around. This is where task priority genuinely wins, absorbing additional DOF where the mode set would need redesign. Concede it.
  • Overhangs are flown over, not under. Safe, but no imagery beneath one and altitude there is measured from the wrong surface. A coverage bound on the method.
  • One terminal state. Slopes too steep to traverse leave the vehicle at the surface, by design, failing safe rather than commanding through terrain.
  • The test terrain is near the edge of feasibility, and the ceiling needs computing properly. A back-of-envelope bound — each 70° tooth costing about 72 s of vertical transit against 35 s of along-track — puts the maximum near 19% at 0.5 m/s, which would make the measured 7.7% roughly 40% of what is attainable. That estimate is too crude to carry the central claim: the offline optimum over the actual terrain is the right instrument, and it is the first item in §8. Report whichever bound is used alongside the result, or the absolute figures read as failure.
